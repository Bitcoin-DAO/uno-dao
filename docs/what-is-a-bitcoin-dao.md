# What is a Bitcoin DAO?

November 2026 · [Italiano](what-is-a-bitcoin-dao.it.md)

A Bitcoin DAO lets a group hold money together and decide together how to spend it, with no single person holding the purse and every decision open for anyone to check.

## What a DAO is

A DAO is a group that shares a pot of money and decides together what to do with it, following rules written down in advance. The name stands for Decentralised Autonomous Organisation, but the idea is older than the name: a club kitty, a village fund, a cooperative's reserve.

What makes a DAO different is who holds the purse. In most groups, one treasurer has the bank card and everyone trusts them. In a DAO, the money can only move when the members have voted for it and several keyholders have signed. No single person can spend it, and everyone can see every proposal, every vote and every payment.

DAOs first appeared on other blockchains, usually with their own tokens. A Bitcoin DAO keeps the same idea and drops everything else: the money is bitcoin, and the rules are kept simple enough to explain at a kitchen table.

## Why build one on Bitcoin

- **The money is real money.** The treasury holds bitcoin, not a token invented for the group. There is nothing to buy in, no price to speculate on, and votes are not weighted by wealth: one member, one vote.
- **Nobody can freeze it.** There is no bank account to close and no company in the middle. The funds sit in a Bitcoin wallet that only the group's keyholders, acting together, can spend from.
- **It outlives the software.** If the website disappeared tomorrow, the keyholders could still recover the money with any standard Bitcoin wallet and a one-page recovery sheet.
- **It stays simple.** Bitcoin does one thing well: hold and move value. The DAO uses it for exactly that, and keeps the decisions in plain proposals that people can read.

## How it works

A Bitcoin DAO has two kinds of people, and keeping them separate is what makes it safe.

| Role | Who | What they do | What they need |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Member | Anyone the group admits | Propose ideas and vote on them | A phone |
| Council member | A small group chosen by the members, five in the first DAO | Approve payments by signing, and confirm changes to the member list and rules | A small signing device kept offline, and their key on a card at home |

Members decide; the council carries out the decision. The council cannot spend without a vote, and members cannot spend without the council. In the first DAO, any three of the five council members must sign before a single satoshi moves.

A payment goes from idea to payment in five steps:

1. **Propose.** A member writes a short proposal: what the money is for, how much, and where it goes. The recipient's Bitcoin address goes in its own box, so it can be checked.
2. **Vote.** Members vote yes, no or abstain for seven days. Votes are signed and public.
3. **Count.** When the vote closes, a council member signs the final count, so nobody can slip in a vote afterwards.
4. **Sign.** Once a month, every approved payment goes out together. Each council member checks the amounts and addresses on their own device and signs. At the third signature the payment is sent.
5. **Record.** The payment appears on the treasury page, linked to its proposal and vote, for anyone to see.

## Taking part

**Joining takes about two minutes.** You need an existing member who knows you to vouch for you, and a council member present, in person or on a video call. On the DAO's website you choose a name (a nickname is fine), confirm with your fingerprint or face, and show a code on your screen. The others scan it and read a short code aloud to make sure it is really you. Members then vote on letting you in.

**There is nothing to remember.** No email, no password. Your phone's fingerprint or face unlock protects your membership, and it follows you on to a new phone if your phone syncs its passkeys.

**You can print an identity card.** It is a small card with a QR code, kept at home like a house key. If you lose your phone, you scan the card and you are back in, with the same name and the same votes. It cannot move any money.

**Voting is a tap.** Open a proposal, read the summary, choose yes, no or abstain. You can change your mind until the vote closes.

**Receiving a payment needs a wallet.** Voting does not, but if the group pays you, you need a Bitcoin wallet of your own to receive it. Free wallet apps take a few minutes to set up.

**You can leave at any time,** with no vote and no explanation. Your past votes stay in the public record.

## What keeps the money safe

- **No one person can spend it.** Three of the five council members must sign every payment. Losing one or two keys does not lose the money, and stealing one or two does not get it.
- **The website can't spend it either.** It holds no keys that move money. It prepares payments and shows the record; only the council's devices can sign.
- **Every council member checks before signing.** Their signing device shows each address and amount on its own screen, so a hacked website cannot quietly change where the money goes.
- **Everything is public.** Members, rules, proposals, votes, the balance and every payment can be checked by anyone, without signing in.
- **It can always be recovered.** A one-page recovery sheet plus any three council keys restores the whole treasury in a standard Bitcoin wallet, with no need for this software or its website.
- **A trial starts small.** The first DAO rehearses twice on Bitcoin's test network, under the name UNO, and then caps its treasury at an amount the community can afford to lose while the system proves itself.

## What it can be used for

A Bitcoin DAO suits any group that shares money, wants everyone to have a say, and makes a handful of payments a month.

- **A local community fund.** A DAO can fund a local bitcoin community: meetups, printed flyers, workshops.
- **Small grants.** Members propose local projects, artists or volunteers to support, and vote on which to fund.
- **Events and festivals.** A shared budget for a yearly event, with every expense proposed and visible.
- **Shared tools and spaces.** A tool library, a community garden or a workshop, with repairs and purchases decided together.
- **Mutual aid.** A fund members can draw on in hard times, with the group deciding each request.
- **Clubs and associations.** A sports club, choir or society that wants an open treasury instead of one treasurer's account.
- **Groups spread across countries.** A diaspora association, an online community or an open-source project, where members meet by video and money needs to cross borders without a bank.
- **Prizes and competitions.** A pooled prize fund paid out when the members' vote picks a winner.

## What it is not

- **Not an investment.** There is no token, no share and no return. Joining costs nothing and earns nothing; it gives you a vote.
- **Not a company or legal body.** A DAO is a way of organising, not a registered entity. What it means legally, for tax or for liability, depends on the country and on what the group does; take local advice before holding significant sums.
- **Not anonymous.** Members can use nicknames, but every vote is public and permanent, and council members are known to each other.
- **Not instant.** Votes take a week and payments go out once a month. That pace is deliberate: it gives people time to read, discuss and check.
- **Not a place for discussion.** Proposals link to wherever the group already talks: a meeting, a chat group, a forum. The DAO records the decision.
- **Not finished.** The software is being tested on Bitcoin's test network. Nothing here is ready for real funds yet.

## Starting your own

Each Bitcoin DAO runs its own copy of the software, with its own website, keys and members. There is no central platform to sign up to and no one to ask permission from. Once the software is released, a group without a programmer will be able to set one up like this:

1. **Agree the basics.** The group's purpose, who sits on the council, how many signatures a payment needs, and the voting rules. A setup page asks these as plain questions.
2. **Hold a key ceremony.** Each council member creates their key with dice and a signing device, keeps it on paper and steel at home, and shares only its public part.
3. **Go live.** The setup produces the group's website and its recovery sheet, ready to publish for free.
4. **Practise first.** Run a round or two on Bitcoin's test network, with play money, before any real funds go in.
5. **Be found, if you want.** A shared directory at btcdao.tech lists DAOs that choose to appear, with their balance and open proposals. It holds no keys and can't change anything.

UNO is the first DAO: it rehearses on the test network, then its council names the DAO that goes to the main network. The release waits until that DAO has run for real and a second group has set up its own from the kit. The design, the specification and the progress are public on [GitHub](https://github.com/Bitcoin-DAO/btc-dao).
